# Bollinger Bands and RSI Reversion

Bollinger Bands and the Relative Strength Index (RSI) are two indicators often studied together to frame mean-reversion conditions. Bollinger Bands consist of a moving average, typically 20 periods, with an upper and lower band placed a set number of standard deviations away, commonly two. Because the bands are built from standard deviation, they widen when volatility rises and contract when it falls, so they describe how far price has stretched relative to its recent typical range. RSI is a momentum oscillator scaled from 0 to 100 that measures the speed and size of recent price changes; readings above roughly 70 are conventionally called overbought and below roughly 30 oversold.

The reversion concept examines what happens when price touches or pokes outside a band at the same time RSI reaches an extreme. A tag of the upper band together with an overbought RSI is studied as a sign that an advance may be stretched, and a tag of the lower band with an oversold RSI as a sign that a decline may be stretched. The two tools are combined precisely because each alone is prone to false readings: a band touch during a strong trend can persist, and RSI can stay extreme for long stretches. Requiring both to align filters some of that noise.

Divergence adds another layer. A bearish divergence occurs when price makes a higher high but RSI makes a lower high, hinting that momentum is not confirming the new extreme; a bullish divergence is the mirror, with a lower low in price and a higher low in RSI. Divergences are studied as early clues that a move is losing energy.

The essential caveat is that these are counter-trend observations. In a durable trend, price can walk along the outer band while RSI remains pinned at an extreme, and reversion signals fail repeatedly. Confirming the broader regime before treating band-and-RSI extremes as reversion setups is a core part of using them responsibly.
