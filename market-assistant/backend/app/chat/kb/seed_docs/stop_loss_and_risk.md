# Stop-Loss Placement and Risk Management

A stop-loss is a predefined level at which a position would be exited to cap the loss on an idea that has not worked out. Its purpose is to convert an open-ended risk into a known, bounded one before any position is taken. Deciding where the stop belongs is therefore part of framing an idea, not an afterthought.

Two common approaches to placement are studied. Structure-based stops sit just beyond a meaningful chart feature, such as below a recent swing low in an uptrend or above a recent swing high in a downtrend. The logic is that if price trades through that structure, the reason for the idea has weakened, so the level marks a natural point of invalidation. Volatility-based stops instead use a measure such as the Average True Range (ATR), placing the stop a multiple of ATR away from the entry reference. Because ATR expands in volatile conditions and contracts in quiet ones, an ATR-based stop adapts its distance to current conditions, giving a calm market a tighter stop and a turbulent one more room.

Risk and reward are assessed together. The risk/reward ratio compares the distance from entry to the stop against the distance from entry to a reasonable objective. A setup where the potential reward is several times the risk is often considered more favourable than one where they are similar, because a positive ratio can leave an approach viable even when only a portion of ideas work out. Stop distance also connects directly to position sizing, since the amount risked per unit determines how large a position keeps total risk within budget.

A recurring discipline is to avoid moving a stop farther away once a position is open. Widening a stop to avoid being taken out converts the predefined, bounded loss into an open-ended one and undermines the entire purpose of setting it. Tightening a stop to protect gains as an idea works is generally viewed differently, but loosening it against the position is the behaviour risk management warns against most.
