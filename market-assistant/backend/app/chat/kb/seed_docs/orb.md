# Opening Range Breakout (ORB)

The Opening Range Breakout is a study of how price behaves after the market open. The "opening range" is defined by the high and low traded during the first N minutes or bars of a session, for example the first 5, 15, or 30 minutes. These early bars often carry unusually heavy volume as overnight orders, news reactions, and positioning are absorbed, so the range they carve out can act as a reference boundary for the rest of the session.

Once the opening range is established, analysts watch for price to move decisively beyond the range high or below the range low. A move above the high is described as an upside breakout, and a move below the low as a downside breakout. The idea being studied is that a clean push past the boundary can signal that one side has taken control and that a directional move may follow. Confirmation is often sought through expanding volume or a bar that closes beyond the level rather than merely piercing it intrabar.

Session anchoring matters because the opening range is only meaningful relative to a specific session's start. On an analysis platform each trading day is anchored to its own open in the exchange's local session, so the range does not bleed across days or across the overnight gap. This keeps the reference levels comparable from one session to the next.

For risk framing, students of ORB commonly reference a stop on the opposite side of the range or a fixed distance inside it, so that a failed breakout that snaps back into the range is identified quickly. Some approaches also note the range width itself as a volatility gauge.

ORB is generally discussed as better suited to sessions that trend away from the open, such as those driven by scheduled news or a strong overnight lead. In quiet, balanced sessions, price may repeatedly cross the boundaries without following through, producing what are called false breakouts. Studying both outcomes helps build realistic expectations.
