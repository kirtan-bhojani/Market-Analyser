# Breakout and Retest

A breakout occurs when price moves through a well-watched level, such as a prior high, a prior low, a trendline, or the edge of a consolidation range. The breakout-retest concept studies what happens immediately afterward: instead of treating the initial break as the whole event, analysts watch for price to return to the broken level and test it from the other side before continuing.

The logic rests on the idea that levels can change roles. A ceiling that price traded beneath for some time, once broken to the upside, may act as a floor on the way back down; the resistance becomes support. Likewise a floor broken to the downside may act as resistance when price rallies back to it. The retest is the moment this role reversal is put to the test. A level that holds on the retest, with price turning away from it in the breakout direction, is often read as confirmation that the move is genuine.

This framework is useful mainly because it offers a way to distinguish real breakouts from false ones. A false breakout, sometimes called a fakeout, is a move that pierces the level, fails to hold, and pulls price back into the prior range. By waiting for the retest rather than reacting to the first push through, analysts trade off some of the earliest movement in exchange for additional evidence that the level has flipped. Confirmation signals that are commonly studied include the retest holding without closing back across the level, a contraction in volatility during the pullback, and renewed volume as price leaves the level again.

The trade-off is explicit: not every genuine breakout retests, so waiting can mean missing moves that run without looking back. Studying both the retests that hold and those that fail helps build a balanced picture of how often the pattern completes versus how often the level gives way entirely.
