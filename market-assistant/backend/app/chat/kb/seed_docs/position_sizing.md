# Position Sizing

Position sizing is the study of how large a position should be relative to the size of an account and the risk taken on a given idea. It is distinct from deciding what to analyse or when; it addresses how much. Many practitioners regard sizing as one of the most influential factors in long-term outcomes because it governs how much of an account is exposed to any single adverse move.

A widely taught framework is fixed-fractional risk. The idea is to define, in advance, a small percentage of account value that one is willing to put at risk on a single position, often cited in educational material as somewhere around one to two percent. This percentage is a risk budget, not the size of the position itself. Keeping it constant means that as an account grows the amount risked per idea grows with it, and as an account shrinks the amount risked contracts, which dampens the impact of losing stretches.

The mechanical link between that risk budget and position size runs through the stop distance. If an analyst has defined a stop level, the distance between the entry reference and that stop expresses how much would be lost per unit if the stop is reached. Dividing the account's risk budget by that per-unit loss gives the size that keeps total risk at the intended fraction. A crucial consequence is that a wider stop implies a smaller size and a tighter stop implies a larger size, so that the amount at risk stays constant regardless of how volatile the instrument is. This ties volatility, stop placement, and size into a single consistent calculation.

The reason sizing protects capital is arithmetic. Losses compound: the larger the drawdown, the larger the subsequent gain required just to recover. By capping the loss on any single idea to a small fraction, fixed-fractional sizing keeps drawdowns survivable and prevents a small number of adverse outcomes from doing disproportionate damage.
