# Market Regimes

A market regime is the prevailing character of price behaviour over a given period. Recognising the regime matters because a method suited to one regime often performs poorly in another, so classifying conditions is a useful first step before applying any analytical framework. Three broad regimes are commonly described: trending, ranging, and high-volatility.

A trending regime is one in which price moves persistently in one direction, forming a sequence of higher highs and higher lows in an uptrend, or lower lows and lower highs in a downtrend. Trends favour continuation-style study such as pullback and breakout analysis. A ranging or balanced regime is one in which price oscillates sideways between a relatively stable ceiling and floor without sustained direction; here mean-reversion and range study are more naturally applicable. A high-volatility regime is characterised by large, fast price swings and wide bars, which can occur within either a trend or a range and which widens stops and enlarges the distance between reference levels.

Two indicators are widely used to help classify regimes. The Average Directional Index (ADX) measures the strength of a trend without regard to its direction; higher readings suggest a stronger directional move while lower readings suggest a directionless, range-like environment. The Average True Range (ATR) measures volatility, capturing how much price is moving per bar on average; a rising ATR points to an expanding, more volatile regime and a falling ATR to a calmer one. Used together, ADX describes how much direction is present and ATR how much movement, giving a two-dimensional read on conditions.

The practical idea being taught is matching the method to the regime. Applying trend-following logic in a quiet range tends to generate repeated false starts, while applying mean-reversion logic in a strong trend fights the dominant flow. Classifying the regime first, then selecting an approach designed for it, is a way of stacking context in one's favour rather than forcing a single method onto every market.
